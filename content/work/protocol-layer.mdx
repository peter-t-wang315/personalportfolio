## The problem

The equipment on the floor doesn't agree on how to talk. Some machines speak
SMEMA handshakes, and three different vendors layer their own proprietary
protocols on top of that. Without a common event shape, every new machine
integration was its own one-off service, and nothing downstream could reason
about "a board moved" the same way twice.

## What I built

An adapter layer: one adapter per vendor protocol, each translating its
machine's native handshake into a common event, normalized by a single
service into the IPC-CFX event schema. Twelve-plus machines, three
protocols, one schema out the other side. A new vendor integration is now an
adapter implementation against a known interface, not a new bespoke service.

The pipeline simulator on the homepage models this exact adapter layer — the
producers, the protocol adapters, and the normalizer are this system,
relabeled in software vocabulary.

## Hard parts

This is the most interesting work on the site.

- **TCP reconnect with exponential backoff and heartbeat-based detection.**
  Machine connections drop. The adapter has to notice — via heartbeat, not
  just a failed write — and back off its reconnect attempts instead of
  hammering a machine that's already struggling.
- **Board-eligibility validation that withholds the SMEMA ready signal.**
  When a board isn't eligible to advance, the adapter doesn't just log a
  warning — it withholds the ready signal back to the machine, which
  physically holds the board in place rather than letting it move forward
  incorrectly. That's a design decision with a physical consequence: get it
  wrong in one direction and a board advances when it shouldn't; get it
  wrong in the other and a healthy line stalls. This is the single most
  interesting thing I've built, and it's the one the simulator's "validation
  hold" button is modeling.

I haven't built message deduplication, idempotency keys, or drop detection
here — where retention or redelivery exists, it's RabbitMQ's queue
durability doing that work, not something I wrote. I'm not claiming
at-least-once or exactly-once delivery anywhere in this system.

## Outcome

New vendor integrations are scoped to writing an adapter against the IPC-CFX
contract, not standing up a new service. The validation-hold behavior has
been running as the correctness backstop for board eligibility across the
protocol layer.

## What I'd do differently

I'd formalize the adapter interface into a proper contract test suite, so a
new adapter can be verified against the schema before it ever touches a real
machine, instead of being validated by hand against the normalizer.
