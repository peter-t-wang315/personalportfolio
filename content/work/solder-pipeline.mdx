## The problem

A selective solder line doesn't solder every board the same way. Depending on
the board model, some units need solder at specific points and some don't
need it at all. That decision, and the physical act of routing a board
through — or around — the solder station, has to happen inline, continuously,
without a person watching every unit.

Before this pipeline existed, that coordination lived as tribal knowledge and
manual routing. It didn't scale past a handful of board models, and it had no
recovery story: if the line hiccuped mid-cycle, there was no defined way back
to a known-good state.

## What I built

Three services, coordinated over RabbitMQ: a camera scan service that reads
the board and determines eligibility, a routing service that decides where
the board goes next, and a machine driver that talks to the solder line over
a TCP socket. Each is a long-running background service — there's no request
a person makes and waits on. The board simply keeps moving, and the pipeline
has to make the correct call at every step or a physical mistake happens.

## Hard parts

- **Partial failure mid-cycle.** If the routing service or the machine driver
  drops out while a board is mid-transit, the pipeline has to know it, not
  silently continue as if nothing happened.
- **Machine timeouts.** The solder line doesn't always respond on the happy
  path. A driver that doesn't distinguish "slow" from "gone" will make the
  wrong call.
- **Bad scans.** A camera read that doesn't map to a known board model has to
  fail safe, not fail silent.
- **Mid-cycle abort.** Stopping a board that's already committed to a solder
  pass without leaving the line in an inconsistent state.

## Outcome

The pipeline runs continuously on the solder line, making the
solder-or-skip call per board without a person in the loop. I'm not citing a
message-rate or defect-rate number here — the site doesn't claim scale that
wasn't measured, and this system's story is correctness under continuous
operation, not throughput.

## What I'd do differently

If the answer above turns out to be "logged and ignored," that's the first
thing I'd change: a malformed message on a live solder line shouldn't just
disappear into a log file. I'd add an explicit dead-letter path with an
operator-visible alert, the same pattern the protocol translation layer uses
for its poison-message handling.
